Dot Lab

Cadmium selenide (CdSe) quantum dots glow when hit with ultraviolet light, and their size alone decides the color. Tune the flask, make the batch messier or more uniform, zap a single dot, then try to match a target color.

What is a quantum dot?

A quantum dot is a tiny crystal of semiconductor material, so small that it contains only a few hundred to a few thousand atoms. Most quantum dots are 2 to 10 nanometers across.

At this scale something unusual happens. In a normal piece of semiconductor, electrons move freely and their energies form continuous bands. Inside a quantum dot, the electron is trapped in all three directions, so its energy becomes quantized: it can only take specific, discrete values.

Why does size control color?

When a quantum dot absorbs ultraviolet (UV) light, an electron jumps to a higher energy level and leaves behind a "hole" (a missing electron). When the electron falls back and recombines with the hole, the dot releases a photon. The photon's energy equals the band gap: the energy difference between the lowest electron level and the highest hole level.

The band gap depends on the size of the dot:

  • Small dots confine the electron more tightly and raise its energy. The band gap widens, so the photon has higher energy and a shorter wavelength: bluer light.
  • Large dots give the electron more room and lower its energy. The band gap shrinks, so the photon has lower energy and a longer wavelength: redder light.

This is the quantum confinement effect, the central principle behind everything in this simulation.

The science behind the numbers

The simulation uses a measured CdSe sizing curve that relates dot diameter to its absorption wavelength, based on real nanocrystal measurements. The band gap is then calculated from the wavelength. Energy and wavelength are the same information, expressed in different units:

E = hcλ

Caveat. Real quantum dots emit slightly redder than their absorption peak, a shift called the Stokes shift. The simulation uses the absorption curve as a close approximation. The physics of confinement

This tab uses effective-mass theory, the simplest model that captures quantum confinement in a spherical dot:

Eg(R) = Eg,bulk + ħ²π²2R²( 1m*e + 1m*h ) − 1.8 e²4πε0ε R

The terms in the equation:

Eg,bulk
1.74 eV, the band gap of bulk CdSe
m*e
0.13 me, the electron effective mass
m*h
0.45 me, the hole effective mass
ε
10.6, the dielectric constant of CdSe

The second term is the confinement energy. It grows as the dot gets smaller, in proportion to 1/R². The third term is the Coulomb attraction between the electron and the hole, a small correction. The model treats the dot as an infinite spherical well, so the walls are assumed to be infinitely high barriers.

The link between energy and wavelength

Band gap and emission wavelength carry the same information:

E(eV) = 1239.84λ(nm)

For example, a 2.4 eV gap emits light at 517 nm, which is green. Watch the two stat boxes at the top flip together as you drag the slider.

What the charts show

Honest limitations. This model is a starting point, not the final word. It works well for larger dots but overestimates the band gap for very small ones, because real dots do not have infinite walls: the barriers are finite and the electron can leak out slightly. Below about 1.5 nm radius, the model predicts ultraviolet emission for CdSe, which real dots do not produce. The wavefunctions shown, ψn(r) ∝ sin(nπr/R)/r, are exact solutions for a particle in a spherical well, but real wavefunctions are modified by the finite barrier and by electron-hole interactions. References: Brus, J. Phys. Chem. 90, 2555 (1986); Norris & Bawendi, Phys. Rev. B 53, 16338 (1996). Wavelength-to-color conversion approximates human vision.

How quantum dots are actually made

The hot-injection method works in three stages:

  1. Precursor preparation. A cadmium precursor is heated in a high-boiling solvent. A selenium precursor is prepared separately.
  2. Injection and nucleation. The selenium precursor is injected all at once into the hot cadmium solution. The selenium concentration spikes far above the nucleation threshold, and in that brief moment many tiny crystals form simultaneously. This burst is the key to a uniform batch: if all the dots are born together, they grow together and end up nearly the same size.
  3. Growth. Once nucleation has used up enough precursor, the concentration drops below the threshold and no new dots form. The existing dots simply grow larger as more material deposits on their surfaces. Taking samples (aliquots) at different times collects dots of different sizes, and therefore different colors.

Why temperature matters

Higher temperature speeds up the diffusion of precursor molecules to the dot surfaces, and reaction rates rise exponentially with temperature. Hotter batches grow faster, so they reach a given size and color sooner.

The growth law

Growth is diffusion-limited. A dot's volume grows roughly linearly with time, so its radius grows as:

R(t) ∝ t1/3

Growth is fast while the dot is small and slows down as it gets bigger.

The LaMer model

The separation of nucleation from growth is described by the LaMer model. If the monomer concentration crosses a critical threshold briefly and then falls below it, nucleation happens in a short pulse and growth continues without any new nucleation. This is the foundation of modern quantum dot synthesis.

What "quench" means

Quenching is rapid cooling, and it stops growth by freezing the diffusion process. Without heat, precursor molecules can no longer reach the dot surfaces, so the size distribution is locked in. This is how you save a particular color during a synthesis.

Why the game mechanics work

  • Growth is not linear. Because R ∝ t1/3, color changes fastest at the start, while dots are small and blue, and slows as they get bigger and redder. That is why the blue end of the spectrum feels crowded: a small change in time produces a large change in color.
